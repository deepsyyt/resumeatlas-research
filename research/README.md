# Research

Each study follows the same structure: the question, how it was measured, what
the data shows, what it means, and — explicitly — what it does not show.

| Study | Question |
| --- | --- |
| [Bullet quantification](resume-bullet-quantification.md) | How many resume bullet points contain a measurable result, and does it matter? |
| [Resume–job alignment](resume-job-alignment.md) | How well do real resumes match the specific postings they are sent to? |
| [Skill gap analysis](skill-gap-analysis.md) | Which required skills are most often absent from resumes? |
| [ATS parsing benchmark](ats-parsing-benchmark.md) | Which parts of a resume break when it is read as a machine-readable document? |

## A note on how to read these

All four studies draw on a **convenience sample**: people who chose to run their
resume through a resume tool. They are not a random sample of job seekers, and
the sample skews toward software, data and engineering roles.

Crucially, **none of this data contains hiring outcomes**. We do not know whether
any resume in the sample led to an interview or an offer. Every finding here is a
statement about the *contents and structure of resumes*, and about how they align
with the *stated requirements of postings* — not about what gets people hired.

Where a study is tempted toward a causal claim, it says so and stops.

Sample sizes differ between studies because different signals were introduced at
different times; each study prints its own `n` and window rather than inheriting a
sitewide figure.
